## What is Atama?

Atama is the starting kit we use at the company to build products faster. It's a white-label base for web and mobile: the features almost every product needs (sign-up, user management, surveys, tasks) already designed, built and tested, plus a design system that any designer can start from and restyle for a client.

A client comes to us with an idea, we check whether Atama already covers the basics, and we get to test a real MVP with real users much sooner. What's left to do is what makes that client's product different.

## At a glance

- **My role:** Product designer. I was assigned the project, but I had a say in every decision about which features to include. I defined how each one should work at its minimum and built the design system under all of them.
- **Scope:** 13 reusable features across web and mobile, and a design system with 1,000+ components.
- **The decision that mattered:** standardize only what most products actually need, and build each feature as a complete module, designed, validated, coded and QA'd.
- **Outcome:** MVPs with basic features went from 2–3 months to under one. Our designers now start from Atama, which has been used in 29 Figma projects.

## Context

Every new product needed the same basics: sign-up, user management, surveys, tasks. And every time, we built them from scratch. That meant design time, development time and QA time. It also pushed back the moment we could put something in front of real users.

Clients were paying for it too. A big part of their budget was going into rework on basic features, instead of the parts that made their product theirs.

So at some point the question was simple: why were we rebuilding the same things?

## What we standardized

The first step was deciding what deserved a place in a reusable base. We went through every project we'd done, the ones still in progress and what clients were asking for, and looked for what kept coming back. For each feature I looked at what we'd built before and what the ideal version should be, then defined the minimum it needed to work inside any product.

I was handed this project, but I wanted to be part of the decisions about what went in. The base had to reflect what we actually needed.

## The design system

Before designing any feature, I built the system that would hold all of them. It follows atomic design, in three layers:

1. **Foundations:** tokens for color, typography, spacing and more, for both web and mobile.
2. **Components:** buttons, inputs and the rest of the building blocks.
3. **Patterns:** headers, modals, tables, cards and other elements made of those components.

It ended up with more than 1,000 components (counting variants), 108 styles and 452 variables.

Customizing it for a client doesn't mean redesigning anything. Color and typography tokens live in the foundations, so changing them updates the whole system. In simple words: a new client's brand is one change, not hundreds.

## Outcomes

By the end of 2024, most of the features were ready to use in Figma, front end and back end. In projects that started in 2025, the difference showed. MVPs with basic features used to take 2–3 months. They now came in under one. With the basics already solved, we could put a working product in front of real users earlier and spend our time on what each client actually needed.

All the designers use the design system as the starting point for any project. 29 Figma projects have been built on Atama.

## What I took away

- **A reusable feature isn't reusable until it's finished.** Designing it wasn't enough. It had to be built and tested too, or the rework just moved somewhere else.
- **Customizing has to be cheap, or nobody does it.** That's why brand changes live in the tokens.
- **Being assigned a project doesn't mean you can't shape it.** The most useful part was having a say in what went in.
